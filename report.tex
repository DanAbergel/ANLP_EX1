\section{Open Questions}

\subsection*{Question 1 -- Three QA datasets that annotate intrinsic concepts}

By ``intrinsic'' I understand datasets where the QA format is just a tool to annotate something about the language, and the answer always sits inside the input sentence -- so you don't need any world knowledge to answer.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{QA-SRL} (He et al., 2015) -- the labels are semantic roles, written as wh-questions about each verb. For \emph{``The pilot landed the plane''} the verb \emph{landed} gives \emph{Who landed something?}~$\rightarrow$ \emph{the pilot} and \emph{What did someone land?}~$\rightarrow$ \emph{the plane}. It's intrinsic because the targets are predicate--argument relations, which are a property of the sentence itself.

    \item \textbf{QAMR} (Michael et al., 2018) -- same idea but more general: the meaning of a sentence is captured by a whole set of free-form QA pairs (arguments, modifiers, etc.). Same reason as QA-SRL for being intrinsic -- the questions describe what the sentence says, not facts about the world.

    \item \textbf{QADiscourse} (Pyatkin et al., 2020) -- here the label is the discourse relation between two clauses (cause, contrast, condition\ldots), encoded as a question whose answer reveals it. Intrinsic because what is being annotated is how the clauses connect inside the text.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Question 2 -- Inference-time scaling methods}

The idea of inference-time scaling is to spend more compute at test time (rather than during training) to get better outputs. From the lecture I noted three methods.

\subsubsection*{(a) The three methods}

\paragraph{1. Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022).}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{Description.} Sample $N$ different chains of thought from the same prompt (with non-zero temperature), then take the majority vote on the final answer.
    \item \emph{Advantages.} Very easy to plug on top of any CoT model -- no extra training, no extra model. Improves accuracy on reasoning tasks pretty reliably.
    \item \emph{Bottlenecks.} You pay roughly $N$ times the decoding cost, and you need to hold $N$ KV caches at the same time.
    \item \emph{Parallelizable?} Yes, easily. The $N$ samples don't depend on each other so you just put them in one big batch.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{2. Verifier-based Best-of-$N$ (Cobbe et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2023).}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{Description.} Same idea (sample $N$ candidates), but instead of voting you score every candidate with a separately trained verifier and keep the one with the best score.
    \item \emph{Advantages.} Can still pick the correct answer when only a minority of the $N$ samples is right -- which majority voting cannot do.
    \item \emph{Bottlenecks.} You pay the $N \times$ generation cost like Self-Consistency, plus $N$ verifier forward passes. Also the verifier itself has to fit in memory next to the generator.
    \item \emph{Parallelizable?} Yes, both the generation and the verifier scoring are independent across the $N$ candidates.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{3. Long chain-of-thought (OpenAI o1, 2024; DeepSeek-R1, 2025).}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{Description.} The model is trained (using RL with a verifiable reward) to produce a single but very long chain of thought, where it plans, backtracks and evaluates itself. We scale the test-time compute by letting it ``think'' for many more tokens.
    \item \emph{Advantages.} Currently the best results on hard reasoning. And there is no external verifier or vote needed at inference time.
    \item \emph{Bottlenecks.} Generation is sequential, so the KV cache and the latency grow with the number of reasoning tokens.
    \item \emph{Parallelizable?} Not inside a single trajectory -- you cannot parallelize an autoregressive decoding. You can still batch across different prompts.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{(b) Single large-memory GPU, complex scientific task}

I would go with the long-CoT model. The reason is that what limits long CoT is the size of the KV cache, and that is exactly what a large-memory GPU provides. The other two methods scale by sampling more candidates, and that benefits more from several GPUs (or just from a higher batch throughput) than from extra memory on a single one. We also saw in the lecture that on hard reasoning tasks (AIME, science olympiads), models that learn to plan and backtrack do better than sample-and-vote methods at the same compute budget.

Self-Consistency would also be a reasonable choice on one GPU, since the $N$ samples can be batched together. But for a hard scientific task, I think one well-trained long chain that plans and self-corrects buys more than voting between $N$ shorter ones.

\section{Programming Exercise -- MRPC Paraphrase Detection}

I fine-tuned \texttt{bert-base-uncased} on MRPC. The whole code is in \texttt{ex1.py} in the repo: it loads the model with \texttt{AutoModelForSequenceClassification}, uses dynamic padding via \texttt{DataCollatorWithPadding}, and logs the loss to W\&B at every step. The W\&B project (3 runs) is here: \url{https://wandb.ai/danabergel1995-hebrew-university-of-jerusalem/anlp-ex1-mrpc}.

\subsection*{Best validation $\Rightarrow$ best test?}

The test accuracies of the three checkpoints are 0.8458, 0.8354 and 0.8365 (in the same order as the table). So the best-on-validation config is also the best on test. One thing I noticed though: the gap between the configs shrinks a lot on the test set (about 1 point, vs.\ 3 points on validation), so the difference between the two non-winning configs on validation is partly noise.

\subsection*{Qualitative analysis: best vs.\ worst configuration}

I compared config 1 (best, 87.0\%) and config 2 (worst, 83.6\%) on the validation set. There are 29 examples where the best is correct and the worst is wrong, and looking at them, two types of cases come back often:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Pairs that share many words but are \emph{not} paraphrases. The worst model seems to rely on word overlap and predicts ``paraphrase''.
    \item Real paraphrases written with different words. The worst model sees little overlap and goes the other way.
\end{itemize}

Two examples below.

\paragraph{Example 1 (gold = 0, worst predicts 1).}
\emph{s1:} ``After losing as much as 84.56 earlier, the Dow Jones industrial average closed up 22.81, or 0.2 percent, at 9{,}340.45.''\\
\emph{s2:} ``In midday trading, the Dow Jones industrial average lost 68.84, or 0.7 percent, to 9{,}248.80.''\\
Both sentences are about the Dow on the same day and use similar words, but all the numbers are different. The worst model gets fooled by the overlap.

\paragraph{Example 2 (gold = 1, worst predicts 0).}
\emph{s1:} ``Broomhead was assigned to 2nd Squadron, 3rd Armor Cavalry Regiment, based at Fort Carson.''\\
\emph{s2:} ``Broomhead, 34, was assigned to the 2nd Squadron, 3rd Armored Cavalry Regiment.''\\
Same fact (same assignment), but s1 mentions Fort Carson and s2 adds his age. Those small added/missing pieces are enough to make the worst model say ``not paraphrase''.

\paragraph{Example 3 (gold = 0, worst predicts 1).}
\emph{s1:} ``\,`It is bad for Symbian,' said Per Lindberg, analyst at Dresdner Kleinwort Wasserstein.''\\
\emph{s2:} ``\,`Motorola has displayed clear disloyalty' to Symbian, said Per Lindberg, an analyst at Dresdner Kleinwort Wasserstein in London.''\\
Same speaker, same affiliation, very similar framing -- but the actual quote in each sentence is different. The worst model seems to anchor on the speaker info and miss that the two reported statements are not the same.
